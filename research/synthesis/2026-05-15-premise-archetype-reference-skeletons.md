## Claim

Premise archetypes get stronger when each one has a small library of cinematic
and television skeletons behind it. The goal is not to copy shows. The goal is
to identify proven story engines that roleplayers already recognize: outsider
entry, town ritual, faction pressure, court succession, hidden identities,
public/private performance, trials, period constraint, and frontier isolation.

For Elbysodic seed data, these references should inform board structure,
canon-roster shape, opening plots, event cadence, face ties, wanted hooks, and
the first scenes a writer can easily imagine.

## How To Use Reference Skeletons

Each seeded community should get:

- One primary reference skeleton: the clearest narrative engine.
- Two secondary reference skeletons: tone, pacing, or relationship structure.
- One anti-reference: a thing the seed should avoid.
- A seed-data translation: locations, factions, canon roles, active plots,
  events, and starting threads.

Do not use names, plots, characters, or protected IP directly. Translate the
mechanics into original premises.

## Archetype Notes

### Small-Town Social Web

Best modern reference: Palm Royale.

Palm Royale is useful because it is not just "rich people in Palm Beach." It
is an outsider-belonging engine: a newcomer tries to enter a closed status
world, the country club acts as the social gate, and every party, outfit,
favor, secret, and snub becomes plot fuel.

Other useful skeletons:
- Gilmore Girls: town calendar, local businesses, recurring eccentrics, and
  multigenerational intimacy.
- Friday Night Lights: one institution makes the town legible; everyone has a
  relationship to the team even when they are not on the field.
- Schitt's Creek: outsiders become locals through work, friendship, and public
  embarrassment.
- Desperate Housewives: neighborhood polish hiding domestic scandal.

Seed-data translation:
- Build a town around three to five social institutions: club, diner, paper,
  school/team, town hall, church, marina, hotel, or hospital.
- Give each canon face a public status and a private pressure.
- Seed seasonal rituals: gala, parade, fundraiser, festival, local election,
  rivalry game, anniversary party.
- Put first-thread hooks in places where people must collide: committee
  meetings, shifts, auditions, tryouts, cleanup days, town emergencies.

Anti-reference:
- Do not make it only cozy. Strong small-town RP needs a public ritual layer
  and a private consequence layer.

### Weird-Town Mystery

Best references: Twin Peaks and Stranger Things.

Twin Peaks proves the durable appeal of a beautiful, ordinary town with a
sinister double life. Stranger Things adds the modern party-of-friends engine:
kids, teens, adults, officials, scientists, and outsiders each hold different
pieces of the mystery.

Other useful skeletons:
- Gravity Falls: clue-of-the-week structure with a larger town mythology.
- Midnight Mass: faith, isolation, and miracles turning into horror.
- Wayward Pines: town rules that only make sense once the hidden system is
  revealed.
- The Leftovers: unexplained event as emotional weather rather than puzzle box
  only.

Seed-data translation:
- Create public rumors, staff-only truths, and partially verified canon.
- Give each location a mundane purpose and a hidden pressure.
- Seed investigators, skeptics, believers, staff insiders, local historians,
  and vulnerable newcomers.
- Use prompts and encounters as mystery pressure, not as railroaded outcomes.

Anti-reference:
- Do not over-answer the mystery in seed data. Writers need enough unknowns to
  contribute.

### Supernatural City

Best references: True Blood and The Vampire Diaries.

True Blood is strong for supernatural publicness: vampires "coming out" creates
law, prejudice, desire, media, religion, work, and violence. The Vampire
Diaries is strong for town history, founding families, forbidden romance, and
species secrets anchored to one place.

Other useful skeletons:
- Buffy and Angel: chosen protectors, Hellmouth-style pressure points, and
  monster-of-the-week plus season-arc design.
- Shadowhunters: formalized supernatural society with hunters, Downworlders,
  laws, and hidden markets.
- The Originals: old families, city power, faction bargains, and inherited
  grudges.
- Being Human: domestic intimacy between supernatural identities.

Seed-data translation:
- Define species, power limits, visibility status, and law/enforcement posture.
- Create factions that need each other but do not trust each other.
- Give the city a nightlife, medical, legal, religious, press, and criminal
  layer.
- Seed current pressure: exposure threat, treaty breach, missing elder, hunter
  campaign, blood shortage, magical disease, faction election.

Anti-reference:
- Do not let species lore become a rulebook wall. Every species page should
  point to playable conflicts.

### Court-And-Faction Fantasy

Best references: Game of Thrones, House of the Dragon, and Shadow and Bone.

The core is not "fantasy world." It is contested legitimacy. Houses, courts,
orders, rebels, priests, mages, borderlands, and monsters all ask who has the
right to rule and what price magic or power demands.

Other useful skeletons:
- Dune: noble houses, ecology, prophecy, extraction, empire.
- The Wheel of Time: chosen-one pressure inside a huge world of institutions.
- The Witcher: monster work, politics, and outsider neutrality under strain.
- The Lord of the Rings: fellowship, ancient evil, and symbolic geography.

Seed-data translation:
- Start with one current crisis: succession, magical depletion, rift, plague,
  occupation, relic theft, border invasion.
- Build five to seven factions with wants, fears, and open seats.
- Use a canon roster for rulers, heirs, spies, commanders, mages, diplomats,
  and outcasts.
- Keep the lore primer short and make all lore serve current play.

Anti-reference:
- Do not seed a lore encyclopedia. Seed a playable political crisis.

### Original Canon-Adjacent AU

Best references: Once Upon a Time, Riverdale, Battlestar Galactica, and
Watchmen.

This archetype needs familiarity without dependency. Once Upon a Time remixes
known archetypes into a town with hidden identities. Riverdale shows how a
familiar cast can be genre-shifted into noir soap. Battlestar Galactica shows
how a premise can be reimagined with a new moral, political, and identity
engine. Watchmen shows how a sequel-world can use inherited canon as history,
not rails.

Seed-data translation:
- Make an original branch point: a war ended differently, a curse moved
  legends into town, a royal line fractured, the academy never fell, the old
  heroes disappeared.
- Provide canon roles as original public archetypes: heir, rebel, prophet,
  exile, rival, traitor, archivist, monster, reformer.
- Allow OCs to enter through institutions, families, factions, and wanted
  hooks.
- Make "inspired-by not IP" explicit.

Anti-reference:
- Do not make wink-and-nod copies. The original premise must stand without the
  audience recognizing the source.

### Spotlight/Fame City

Best references: Palm Royale, Mad Men, and The Marvelous Mrs. Maisel.

Palm Royale brings high-society gatekeeping. Mad Men brings public image,
professional ambition, hidden personal history, and era-specific pressure.
Maisel brings performance spaces, career ladders, gender expectations, managers,
bookers, critics, family shame, and public reinvention.

Other useful skeletons:
- Nashville: artists, labels, touring, rivalry, family, and changing industry.
- Feud: Capote vs. The Swans: access journalism, betrayal, elite gossip, and
  social exile.
- Hollywood: dream factory, gatekeeping, image-making, and reinvention.

Seed-data translation:
- Build career tracks: actor, musician, athlete, designer, publicist,
  journalist, producer, club owner, influencer, legacy socialite.
- Seed public/private scene flags.
- Create rumor/news objects, PR crises, awards, premieres, benefits, and
  industry parties.
- Give characters an image they sell and a truth they protect.

Anti-reference:
- Do not build a generic celeb board. The premise needs an institution:
  studio, club, label, magazine, venue circuit, or high-society season.

### Trial Clans Or Academy

Best references: The 100, Yellowjackets, The Hunger Games, and Squid Game.

The engine is forced structure under pressure: teams, clans, districts, ranks,
trials, scarcity, selection, leadership, and moral compromise. The 100 is
useful for clans and survival politics. Yellowjackets is useful for team
collapse and long-tail trauma. Hunger Games and Squid Game show ritualized
competition as social control.

Seed-data translation:
- Define the institution: clans, academy houses, districts, trials, teams,
  orders, packs, or crews.
- Seed rank openings and obligations.
- Create task prompts and consequences that affect the group, not only one
  thread.
- Make fairness and consent rules explicit before any danger mechanics.

Anti-reference:
- Do not make every plot life-or-death. Daily obligations, rivalries, and
  resource strain sustain more RP than constant elimination.

### Occult Historical City

Best references: Peaky Blinders, Bridgerton, Deadwood, and Carnival Row.

Historical pressure is strongest when the city has institutions. Peaky Blinders
uses family, crime, class, postwar trauma, business, and politics. Bridgerton
uses the social season, marriage market, etiquette, gossip, and family
reputation. Deadwood uses a settlement becoming a town. Carnival Row uses a
Victorian-like fantasy city with immigration, prejudice, murders, and political
tension.

Seed-data translation:
- Pick one period pressure: industrialization, reform, war aftermath, occult
  panic, migration, plague, empire, class mobility, suffrage, crime.
- Create institutions: newspaper, police, courts, clubs, church, union,
  hospital, theater, docks, occult society.
- Provide inclusive adaptation rules and content warnings.
- Use occult elements to heighten social stakes, not erase period constraints.

Anti-reference:
- Do not reproduce oppressive history as ambience. The seed needs clear safety
  posture and writer-facing boundaries.

### Strange Frontier

Best references: Firefly, The Expanse, Silo, and Westworld.

The strange frontier is about isolation and control. Firefly provides crew,
cargo, illegal work, found family, and frontier law. The Expanse provides
factions, labor, class, and political tension across a frontier system. Silo
provides an enclosed society with forbidden knowledge. Westworld provides a
constructed frontier, memory control, and role/script rebellion.

Seed-data translation:
- Define the frontier: desert station, mining town, ship, outpost, silo,
  theme-park colony, alien-contact zone, terraforming settlement.
- Seed jobs that force interaction: mechanic, medic, marshal, smuggler,
  engineer, archivist, surveyor, preacher, trader, scientist, performer.
- Create a control mystery: memory loss, sealed levels, missing convoy,
  corporate lie, alien signal, broken terraforming system.
- Give the map scarcity: water, air, fuel, medicine, food, passage, data.

Anti-reference:
- Do not make hard sci-fi homework. The seed should sell place, labor, and
  mystery first.

## Product Implications

Accepted:
- Use cinematic/television skeletons to enrich each premise archetype before
  expanding seed data.
- Keep all seeded communities premise-based.
- Use wanted hooks only as supporting connective tissue inside each premise.
- Exclude sanctuary sandboxes from the curated initial seed slate.

Proposed:
- For each seeded community, define `reference_skeletons` in the seed-planning
  artifact before writing data.
- Create original canon rosters with archetypal roles derived from skeletons:
  outsider, gatekeeper, heir, rival, investigator, reformer, exile, operator,
  social climber, reluctant protector, public face, secret keeper.
- Use references to design first-board layout: institution boards, event
  boards, private/staff truths, public rumor surfaces, and starting threads.

Rejected:
- Copying titles, characters, places, plots, or protected IP.
- Treating reference skeletons as genre tags only.
