# Elbysodic Logo Exploration

Date: 2026-05-10

Status: archived exploration. The selected Circle Terminals brand kit now lives
in `design/brand/circle-terminals/`; production app assets live in
`src/elbysodic/web/static/brand/`.

This is a research-backed starting set for Elbysodic logo direction. It is not
a final brand decision or production asset set. The goal is to test symbolic
territory before choosing one direction to refine.

## Research Takeaways

Paul Rand's 1991 AIGA essay argues that a logo identifies rather than explains,
and that effectiveness depends on distinctiveness, visibility, usability,
memorability, universality, durability, and timelessness. The strongest
implication for Elbysodic is that the logo should not try to depict an entire
forum, thread, character sheet, or writing product. It should become a clear
signature for the studio layer over time.

Mid-century American modernism gives us a useful formal toolkit: elemental
typography, primary or high-chroma color, photography, geometric forms, and
biomorphic forms. Cooper Hewitt's summary of `The Moderns` frames the movement
as a broad postwar visual language shaped by European emigres and American
designers, not a single nostalgic style. For Elbysodic, that suggests a logo
that is structured and memorable without becoming retro pastiche.

Saul Bass is useful less as a visual style to copy and more as a model for
compression: AIGA Eye on Design summarizes Bass's work as distilling a story
into a single image. Elbysodic should do the same for PBP: character identity,
scene continuity, and director control compressed into a mark.

Alexander Girard's mid-century lettering and pattern work is a good warning and
opportunity. Cooper Hewitt notes that his 1960 `Alphabet` textile is gridded
and organic, orderly and irregular. That tension maps well to Elbysodic:
structured rosters and threads supporting messy human writing.

The existing Elbysodic design doctrine adds a second constraint: technicolor
futurism. The logo should use a black-key structure with controlled dye-record
accents, not broad neon spectacle. The mark should survive one-color use, small
sizes, app icons, favicons, social avatars, and director-customized themes.

## Concept Options

1. `registered-thread-monogram.svg`
   - A modular E built from registered thread bars.
   - Best if the brand should feel precise, editorial, and product-native.
   - Strongest small-size candidate.

2. `scene-aperture-roster.svg`
   - A circular aperture around a quiet thread core and roster dots.
   - Best if the brand should foreground scene framing, face identity, and
     cinematic story control.
   - Softer and more human, but more complex at favicon size.

3. `face-aperture.svg`
   - A rounded studio aperture with an abstract face/profile cut.
   - Best if the brand should foreground active face, pseudonymous identity,
     and public authorship.
   - Needs careful refinement so it does not read as a generic avatar app.

4. `director-registration.svg`
   - A compact crop/registration mark around a central scene dot.
   - Best if the brand should emphasize director control, color-score
     governance, and precise studio tooling.
   - Strong one-color candidate.

5. `page-signal-escutcheon.svg`
   - A shield/page/signature device with a signal cut.
   - Best if the brand should feel like a trusted studio badge or director
     mark.
   - Most aligned with Rand's flag/signature/escutcheon framing.

6. `casting-constellation.svg`
   - A mid-century starburst with connected face nodes.
   - Best if the brand should emphasize casting, wanted hooks, roster energy,
     and community signal.
   - Most expressive option, with the highest risk of reading as event-only.

7. `wordmark-lockup.svg`
   - A first-pass lockup using the registered-thread mark plus a plain
     geometric wordmark.
   - Best for evaluating name rhythm and spacing beside a compact mark.
   - The text should be custom drawn before production use.

initial options on a dark contact sheet.

## Second-Pass Variants

These build on the strongest first-round directions.

1. `face-thread-aperture.svg`
   - Keeps the face aperture but lets thread bars pass through the identity
     frame.
   - More PBP-specific than the first face mark because it ties public
     authorship to scene continuity.

2. `dual-face-aperture.svg`
   - Two opposing abstract profiles inside one aperture.
   - Tests the global account/community membership/character identity tension
     without becoming literal.

3. `director-aperture-grid.svg`
   - A stricter production-room grid with a central scene target.
   - More operational and studio-like than the first registration mark.

4. `color-score-registration.svg`
   - A registration target built from four color-score arcs.
   - Best fit for Appearance Studio and director-authored art direction.

5. `casting-orbit.svg`
   - Wanted signal plus roster orbit.
   - More playable and social than the first constellation, while still
     avoiding generic chat/community bubbles.

6. `roster-signal-star.svg`
   - A compact star/device with four face nodes and a central thread signal.
   - Stronger app-icon candidate than `casting-constellation.svg`, but less
     explicitly director/studio.

## Third-Pass Negative-Space Variants

These test whether the identity can be carried mostly by counterform instead
of additive detail.

1. `negative-face-e.svg`
   - A block E whose counterform creates an abstract face/profile.
   - Good bridge between monogram, active face, and small-size recognition.

2. `negative-thread-cut.svg`
   - Thread bars are cut by a dark studio spine and forward scene arrow.
   - Strong for app icon use; less emotionally human.

3. `negative-director-mask.svg`
   - A registration frame where the cutout becomes a face mask.
   - Best blend of director control and pseudonymous identity.

4. `negative-casting-slot.svg`
   - A casting star with a negative posting slot at the center.
   - Keeps wanted/roster energy while adding a writing primitive.

5. `negative-roster-aperture.svg`
   - Aperture ring with an E-shaped roster path in counterform.
   - Most systematic and simple of the negative-space set.

6. `negative-wordmark-glyph.svg`
   - Wordmark study where the `o` becomes a negative-space scene aperture.
   - Useful as a lockup idea, not a finished type treatment.

## Fourth-Pass Thread-Cut Refinements

These move directly toward `negative-thread-cut.svg`, with less symbolic
branching and more production-logo pressure.

1. `thread-cut-refined.svg`
   - Cleaner proportions and quieter accent rails.
   - Best current refinement target if the original direction remains the
     favorite.

2. `thread-cut-solid.svg`
   - Strict one-color test.
   - Useful for favicon, stamp, embroidery, print, and loading glyph checks.

3. `thread-cut-face.svg`
   - Tests whether the dark spine can hint at active face identity.
   - More Elbysodic-specific, but slightly more complex.

4. `thread-cut-gate.svg`
   - More decisive scene-gate shape with stronger forward motion.
   - Best if the mark should feel like entering a scene or board.

5. `thread-cut-roster.svg`
   - Adds roster nodes to the left edge.
   - More PBP vocabulary, less pure monogram.

6. `thread-cut-badge.svg`
   - Badge/shield container for app icon use.
   - Strong but may feel more institutional than studio-native.

7. `thread-cut-lockup.svg`
   - Wordmark spacing study using the refined mark.
   - Text is still placeholder system type, not a final custom wordmark.

## Fifth-Pass Lockup And Roster Refinements

   - Tighter version of the lockup with a cleaner subtitle and color rails.
   - Best candidate for product-header use.

2. `thread-cut-lockup-b.svg`

5. `thread-cut-roster-b.svg`

   - Stronger one-color logic, quieter personality.

## Sixth-Pass E / Technicolor Hollywood Variants

These lean harder into the E itself while borrowing from Technicolor futurism
and Golden Age or Platinum Age Hollywood: title cards, studio seals, spotlight
apertures, marquee rhythm, and registered dye records. The goal is controlled
cinema grammar, not retro theater decoration.

1. `e-technicolor-gate.svg`
   - Closest to the thread-cut family, but with a stronger studio-gate read.
   - Good candidate if the E should remain the product's primary mark.

2. `e-platinum-monogram.svg`
   - Circular platinum-era studio monogram treatment.
   - More prestigious and formal; less directly PBP.

3. `e-title-card.svg`
   - Old-Hollywood title-card frame reduced into a sharp E device.
   - Useful for splash screens and launch branding.

4. `e-spotlight-aperture.svg`
   - Spotlight beam intersects with the E/thread structure.
   - Most cinematic, but may need simplification for small sizes.

5. `e-cinema-prism.svg`
   - The thread-cut E splitting into cyan, magenta, and amber light records.
   - Strongest Technicolor futurism bridge.

6. `e-marquee-roster.svg`
   - Marquee lights reinterpreted as roster/face signals.
   - Warmer and more theatrical; watch for decorative drift.

7. `e-studio-seal-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup with an E seal and restrained editorial wordmark.
   - Best direct comparison against `thread-cut-lockup-a.svg`.

## Seventh-Pass Lowercase e Variants

These test whether a lowercase `e` can keep the thread-cut identity while
feeling softer, more aperture-like, and more compatible with the lowercase
wordmark.

1. `lowercase-e-thread-cut.svg`
   - Closest lowercase translation of the current thread-cut direction.
   - Good if the mark should feel friendlier without losing structure.

2. `lowercase-e-aperture.svg`
   - More cinematic, with the `e` as an aperture/scene lens.
   - Better for studio identity than small favicon use.

3. `lowercase-e-face.svg`
   - Counterform leans into active-face identity.
   - Most PBP-specific lowercase option, but more complex.

4. `lowercase-e-prism.svg`
   - Softer `e` with Technicolor prism records.
   - Strong bridge to the futurist cinema direction.

5. `lowercase-e-roster.svg`
   - Adds face nodes to the lowercase mark.
   - Good comparison against `thread-cut-roster-a.svg`.

6. `lowercase-e-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup using the lowercase mark.
   - Best direct comparison against `thread-cut-lockup-a.svg`.

## Eighth-Pass Lowercase e / Three-Strip Color Records

These make the Technicolor camera metaphor explicit: the `e` becomes a
beam-split or registered three-record mark, with each section carrying one of
the cyan, magenta, and amber dye roles.

1. `lowercase-e-three-strip.svg`
   - The `e` itself is divided into cyan, magenta, and amber sections.
   - Closest to the user's "each section one color" prompt.

2. `lowercase-e-prism-records.svg`
   - Keeps a white lowercase `e` but shows color records passing through it.
   - More readable as a single mark; less color-forward.

3. `lowercase-e-separated-records.svg`
   - Builds the `e` from three separated color bands.
   - Strongest print/registration analogy.

4. `lowercase-e-beamsplitter.svg`
   - Adds a central prism shape and three branching records.
   - Most literal Technicolor camera reference.

5. `lowercase-e-color-record-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup using the segmented lowercase `e`.
   - Useful to compare against both `lowercase-e-lockup.svg` and
     `thread-cut-lockup-a.svg`.

## Online Size Candidates

The earlier exploratory marks are intentionally broad. These variants are
stripped down for real online use: favicon, app icon, mobile header, social
avatar, and wordmark-adjacent contexts.

1. `online-e-prism.svg`
   - Simplified lowercase `e` with three color-record regions.
   - Best if the Technicolor concept should be obvious at medium sizes.

2. `online-thread-e.svg`
   - White lowercase `e` with tiny dye-record accents.
   - Best balance of lowercase friendliness and small-size recognition.

3. `online-thread-cut.svg`
   - Simplified version of the strongest uppercase thread-cut family.
   - Best favicon/app-icon silhouette.

4. `online-roster-cut.svg`
   - Thread-cut silhouette plus three face nodes.
   - Best if roster/face identity must appear in the mark, but more fragile at
     16px.

Open `online-logo-test.html` for a focused size stress test.

## Ninth-Pass Four-Part Segmented e

These follow the specific direction of a lowercase `e` sliced into four parts:
cyan, magenta, yellow, and a green transition/tail shape.

1. `segmented-e-four-part.svg`
   - Direct four-section lowercase `e`.
   - Best literal response to the segmented-color idea.

2. `segmented-e-tail.svg`
   - Green section becomes the tail and forward scene path.
   - Strongest connection to the thread-cut motion language.

3. `segmented-e-aperture.svg`
   - Same segmented `e` inside an aperture/studio seal.
   - Better for app icon use, less wordmark-friendly.

4. `segmented-e-lockup.svg`
   - Wordmark comparison for the segmented `e`.
   - Useful to judge whether the mark can live in header/product chrome.

## Twelfth-Pass Circle Terminals

These correct the circle direction so the circles are the actual left
terminals: `o ---`, `o -`, `o ---`.

1. `roster-cut-circle-terminals.svg`
   - Colored circle terminals followed by bars.
   - Softer and more roster/face-like than square color blocks.

2. `roster-cut-circle-terminals-arrow.svg`
   - Same terminal system with the intentional forward arrow cut.
   - More scene motion, less pure.

3. `roster-cut-circle-terminals-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup for the circle-terminal version.

## Thirteenth-Pass Circle Terminal Overlap

These test whether the selected circle-terminal direction can become more
integrated by letting circles overlap, bleed into, or register against the
thread bars.

1. `circle-terminals-overlap-soft.svg`
   - Circles gently overlap the bars.
   - Likely the safest overlap variant.

2. `circle-terminals-overlap-bleed.svg`
   - Color bleeds farther into the bars.
   - More Technicolor-record feeling, but may get tight at 16px.

3. `circle-terminals-overlap-tight.svg`
   - Compresses vertical spacing.
   - Useful stress case; likely too tight for primary use.

4. `circle-terminals-overlap-registered.svg`
   - Slight registration offsets at the circle/bar join.
   - More historical Technicolor energy, less clean.

5. `circle-terminals-overlap-cut.svg`
   - Overlap gets cut back by the black spine.
   - Crisp and distinctive, but denser.

6. `circle-terminals-overlap-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup using the soft-overlap direction.

## Fourteenth-Pass Vertical Circle Overlap

These test the Mastercard-like idea in the correct axis: the colored terminals
merge vertically with each other, while the thread bars remain long, short,
long.

1. `circle-terminals-vertical-overlap.svg`
   - Three larger discs overlap vertically.
   - Cleanest interpretation of the vertical-overlap request.

2. `circle-terminals-vertical-merge.svg`
   - Adds visible transition colors where discs meet.
   - More expressive, less small-size stable.

3. `circle-terminals-vertical-intercut.svg`
   - Uses black-key cuts between overlapping discs.
   - Better small-size separation, less merged.

4. `circle-terminals-vertical-column.svg`
   - Turns the overlap into a continuous color column.
   - Strong but may drift away from the `o ---` structure.

5. `circle-terminals-vertical-lockup.svg`
   - Lockup using the clean vertical-overlap direction.
